# The cash flow statement

The cash flow statement explains the period's movement in cash, not profit. It reclassifies every cash event into three sections - operating, investing, financing - and, prepared correctly, the net of those three sections (plus any separate FX-effect line for multi-currency groups) must equal the movement in the cash and cash-equivalents balance on the balance sheet. It answers a question the P&L cannot: where did cash actually come from and go.

## The three sections
Every cash flow belongs to exactly one section, by the nature of the activity:
- Operating - cash from the trade itself: receipts from customers, payments to suppliers and staff, and (commonly) interest and tax. This is the engine; sustained negative operating cash flow is one of the strongest warning signs in the statement - though it is normal and expected for many early-stage or rapidly growing businesses, so read it in context rather than as an automatic red flag.
- Investing - cash for acquiring/disposing of long-term assets and other-entity investments: buying/selling property, plant, equipment, intangibles, subsidiaries, and investment securities.
- Financing - cash between the entity and its funders: drawing/repaying loans, issuing/buying back shares, paying dividends.
Classify by substance, not by which ledger account moved. Buying a machine for cash is investing even though cash (an operating-looking account) fell.

## Cash and cash-equivalents - what ties
The statement reconciles to "cash and cash-equivalents", not just the current account. Cash-equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to known amounts of cash and subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value - conventionally an original maturity of three months or less, measured from the date the entity acquires the instrument (not from the issuer's original issue date) (e.g. some money-market funds, short T-bills). Under IFRS (IAS 7), bank overdrafts repayable on demand are included as a component of cash and cash-equivalents only when they also form an integral part of the entity's cash management (fluctuating between debit and credit); this is a facts-and-circumstances judgement, so confirm the entity's definition. US GAAP differs: overdrafts are not netted into cash - they are short-term financing, and the change in the overdraft balance is presented as a financing activity. The closing total here must equal the cash and cash-equivalents line on the balance sheet.

## Direct vs indirect method (operating section only)
Both produce the SAME operating cash figure; only the presentation of the operating section differs (investing and financing are identical either way).
- Direct method: lists actual operating cash flows - cash received from customers, cash paid to suppliers, cash paid to employees, etc. Argued by some to be more informative, but less common, because it needs cash-basis data the ledger does not naturally produce (and whether it is genuinely more decision-useful is contested, which is why no major framework mandates it).
- Indirect method: starts from profit and adjusts back to cash. By far the most common in practice. The rest of this doc focuses on it.

## The indirect method - building the operating section
Start from profit (commonly profit before tax, or operating profit, depending on layout; note IFRS 18 will require the operating profit subtotal as the starting point for periods from 1 January 2027) and apply three adjustment groups to reconcile profit to operating cash:
1. Add back non-cash expenses charged in arriving at profit: depreciation, amortisation, impairment, increases in provisions, share-based payment, loss on asset disposal. These reduced profit but moved no cash, so add them back.
2. Remove items that belong in another section: deduct interest income and gains on disposal, add back interest/finance expense (if interest is shown elsewhere) - because the cash sits in investing/financing, not operating.
3. Adjust for working-capital movements (see next section).
The logic: profit is accrual-based; you are stripping out everything non-cash and everything that is not operating, to land on cash actually generated by operations.

## Working-capital movements - the direction rule
This is where most errors occur. The rule for the indirect method:
- Receivables (debtors) UP -> SUBTRACT. You earned revenue (in profit) but the cash is still owed, so cash is lower than profit. Receivables DOWN -> add (you collected old debts).
- Inventory UP -> SUBTRACT. Cash spent building stock that is not yet expensed through COGS. Inventory DOWN -> add.
- Payables (creditors) UP -> ADD. You incurred cost (in profit) but have not paid it, so cash is higher than profit. Payables DOWN -> subtract (you settled old bills).
Tell: an increase in an operating ASSET is a use of cash (subtract); an increase in an operating LIABILITY is a source of cash (add). Use the movement BETWEEN balance-sheet dates, and exclude movements driven by non-cash or investing/financing events (e.g. a payable for a fixed asset is investing, not working capital).

## Interest, tax and dividends - placement
Placement varies by framework, so be explicit and consistent period to period.
- IFRS (classic IAS 7) allows choice: interest paid/received and dividends received may sit in operating OR financing/investing; dividends paid in financing or operating. Whatever is chosen must be applied consistently and disclosed. Note: IFRS 18 narrows this freedom for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027 by classifying these items by category, so check which regime applies.
- US GAAP is stricter: interest paid/received and dividends received are operating; dividends paid are financing.
- Tax paid is normally operating under both, unless specifically identifiable with an investing or financing activity.
The cash figure is what flows in the statement (tax PAID, not the P&L tax charge); the difference between the charge and the cash is a working-capital-style movement in the tax payable balance.

## The reconciliation that must hold
Operating cash flow + investing cash flow + financing cash flow = net increase/(decrease) in cash and cash-equivalents. Then: opening cash and cash-equivalents + that net movement = closing cash and cash-equivalents, which must equal the balance-sheet cash line. If it does not tie, the statement is wrong - do not publish it. For multi-currency groups, the effect of exchange-rate changes on cash held is shown as a separate reconciling line (not within the three sections) so the movement still ties to the balance sheet.

## Common mistakes
- Classifying interest or tax in the wrong section -> overstates/understates operating cash and breaks comparability. Right move: confirm the framework (IFRS choice vs US GAAP rule), apply it consistently, and disclose the policy.
- Forgetting a non-cash add-back (depreciation, impairment, provision movements, share-based payment) -> operating cash comes out too low and the statement will not tie. Right move: reconcile every non-cash line in the P&L and provisions note back into the adjustments.
- Getting the working-capital sign backwards (e.g. adding a rise in receivables) -> the classic indirect-method error. Right move: asset up = subtract, liability up = add; sanity-check that collecting cash improves operating cash.
- Using P&L tax/interest instead of cash paid -> mixes accrual and cash figures. Right move: take the cash actually paid; the accrual-vs-cash gap is the movement in the related payable.
- Mixing sections - e.g. putting proceeds from a share issue in operating, or asset-purchase cash in operating -> distorts every subtotal. Right move: classify by the nature of the activity, not the ledger account.
- Not tying to the balance-sheet cash movement -> the single most important check, often skipped. Right move: prove opening cash + net movement (+ FX effect) = closing cash = balance-sheet cash before sign-off; an unexplained residual means an error, never a plug.
- Including non-cash transactions (e.g. asset bought on finance lease, debt-for-equity swap) in the body -> these moved no cash. Right move: exclude from the three sections and disclose separately as non-cash investing/financing.
