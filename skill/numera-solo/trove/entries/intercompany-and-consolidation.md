# Intercompany transactions and consolidation

How transactions between members of the same group are recorded, reconciled, and removed when the group reports as one. The elimination mechanics below are broadly common across frameworks (IFRS 10 / ASC 810 and most local GAAP), but measurement details (notably how NCI is measured), the control/scope tests that decide what gets consolidated, and certain local consolidation exemptions differ, so confirm against the applicable standard before relying on specifics.

## The group is one economic entity
Consolidated accounts present a parent and its subsidiaries as if they were a single entity. The cardinal rule follows from that: a group cannot trade, lend, or owe money to itself. Anything that happens purely between group members is internal and must be ELIMINATED before the consolidated statements are produced. The standalone (entity-level) books keep the intercompany entries in full; elimination happens only at the consolidation layer, never by deleting the entry from a subsidiary's own ledger. An AI must keep these two layers distinct: entity ledgers stay intact, the consolidation adds eliminating entries on top.

## Intercompany balances must reconcile before they eliminate
Every intercompany balance has two sides held by two different entities, and they must mirror exactly. If Sub A shows an intercompany receivable from Sub B of 50,000, Sub B must show an intercompany payable to Sub A of 50,000. The elimination only nets to zero if both sides agree, so reconciling intercompany balances is a hard precondition for consolidation, not a courtesy. Mismatches come from timing (one side booked this period, the other next), FX (each side translating a foreign intercompany balance at a different rate), or a plain missed entry. Reconcile and agree both sides first; eliminate second. A residual after elimination means the two sides never matched.

## What gets eliminated
Pair the two sides and remove both legs. Intercompany sales/purchases: DR group revenue / CR group cost of sales for the full internal trading value, so neither inflates the consolidated P&L. Intercompany receivables/payables: DR intercompany payable / CR intercompany receivable, removing both balances from the consolidated balance sheet. Intercompany loans: eliminate the loan asset against the loan liability, and eliminate the matching interest income against interest expense. Intercompany dividends: where the parent carries the investment at cost or fair value, the subsidiary's dividend paid to the parent shows as income in the parent (under the equity method the dividend instead reduces the carrying amount of the investment); on consolidation eliminate the intra-group dividend (DR dividend income / CR dividend declared) so group equity is not double-counted. None of these are real to an outside party, so none survive consolidation.

## Why intercompany must net to zero across the group
Internal trade creates equal and opposite entries inside the group: one entity's revenue is another's cost, one's asset is another's liability, one's income is another's expense. Summed across the whole group they cancel to zero by construction. So after elimination the consolidated total of every intercompany account, receivable, payable, sales, purchases, interest, dividends, must be exactly nil. A non-zero intercompany total in the consolidated figures is a defect: either a balance was eliminated on one side only, or the two sides never reconciled. This zero-sum check is the single best test that consolidation was done correctly.

## Unrealised profit in stock
When one group member sells inventory to another at a margin and the buyer still holds it at period-end, the selling entity has booked a profit the group has not actually earned (the goods are still inside the group, not sold to a third party). That margin is unrealised and must be eliminated: DR consolidated cost of sales (reducing group profit) / CR group inventory (writing the stock back down to original group cost). Only the profit on stock STILL on hand is eliminated; once the goods are sold onward to an outside customer the profit becomes real and is left alone. The same principle extends to internal profit sitting in fixed assets transferred within the group, with one added step for depreciable assets: the eliminated profit must be unwound over the asset's remaining life through an adjustment to the buyer's excess depreciation (the buyer depreciates the marked-up cost, so each period a portion of the unrealised profit is realised and reversed).

## Minority / non-controlling interest (NCI)
When the parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the group still consolidates 100% of that subsidiary's assets, liabilities, income, and expenses, then carves out the share that belongs to outside shareholders as non-controlling interest. NCI appears as its own line within equity on the balance sheet, and the subsidiary's profit is split on the P&L between the amount attributable to the parent's owners and the amount attributable to NCI. NCI is not a liability and not the parent's, it is the outsiders' slice of a fully consolidated subsidiary. Intercompany balances and trading are still eliminated in full regardless of the ownership percentage. One caveat on unrealised profit: where the selling entity is the subsidiary (an upstream sale), eliminating its unrealised profit reduces the subsidiary's profit that is itself split, so that elimination is shared between the parent's owners and NCI in proportion to ownership; downstream unrealised profit (parent sells to subsidiary) is borne by the parent alone. Otherwise the NCI split is a separate step applied to the subsidiary's results.

## Common mistakes
- Leaving intercompany un-eliminated. Why wrong: consolidated revenue, costs, assets, and liabilities are inflated by internal trading that has no external counterparty, overstating the group. Right move: pair and eliminate every intercompany account so each nets to zero in the consolidated totals.
- Mismatched intercompany balances. Why wrong: if the two sides do not agree, elimination leaves a non-zero intercompany residual (the difference falls into P&L or a reconciliation/suspense difference). Note this does not make the consolidated balance sheet fail to balance, since the consolidation is still double-entry, it just fails the net-to-zero check and distorts the figures. Right move: reconcile both legs (chase timing and FX differences) and agree them before eliminating, never plug the gap to suspense.
- Double counting. Why wrong: eliminating only one side, or eliminating at the entity level AND again at consolidation, removes too little or too much. Right move: keep entity ledgers whole and apply each elimination once, at the consolidation layer only.
- Forgetting unrealised profit on closing stock. Why wrong: internal margin on goods still held inside the group is recognised before the group has earned it, overstating profit and inventory. Right move: DR cost of sales / CR inventory for the margin on stock still on hand at period-end.
- Treating NCI as a liability or netting it off. Why wrong: NCI is the outsiders' equity in a fully consolidated subsidiary, not debt and not the parent's. Right move: consolidate 100% then present NCI separately within equity and split profit attributable to NCI on the P&L.
